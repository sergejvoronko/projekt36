The E36 chassis is a masterpiece of 90s engineering, but its suspension geometry was designed for stock ride heights and relatively soft bushings. Once you lower your car and start pushing it on the street, track, or drift course, those factory settings become a major limiting factor. Incorrect geometry not only accelerates tire wear but actively works against the grip and response you're trying to achieve.

This guide breaks down the essential geometry adjustments for the E36 platform. We'll cover the parts you need, why you need them, and provide specific alignment specs to transform your car's handling for your chosen discipline.

## Why Stock Geometry Fails When Lowered

The E36 uses a MacPherson strut front and a "Z-Axle" multi-link rear suspension. When you install coilovers or lowering springs, the car's static position in its suspension travel changes dramatically, which has several negative consequences:

1.  **Excessive Negative Camber:** As the suspension compresses (i.e., you lower the car), both the front and rear wheels gain negative camber. While some is good, too much will destroy the inside edge of your tires during daily driving and can reduce the contact patch under braking.
2.  **Toe Changes:** Lowering the E36 causes the front wheels to toe out and the rear wheels to toe in. Front toe-out creates a darty, unstable feeling on the highway, while excessive rear toe-in can cause sluggish turn-in and scrubbing.
3.  **Bump Steer:** The relationship between the steering tie rod and the lower control arm is altered. On a significantly lowered E36, the wheel can toe in or out on its own as the suspension compresses over bumps, making the car feel unpredictable and nervous.
4.  **Limited Factory Adjustment:** The E36 has very little built-in adjustment. The front has no factory camber or caster adjustment (some models used eccentric "crash bolts" for tiny corrections), and the rear has a small amount of camber and toe adjustment via eccentric bolts. This is insufficient for any performance-oriented ride height.

To fix this, we need to install adjustable components that give us control over these critical angles.

## Front Axle: Dialing in Front-End Grip

The front end dictates turn-in, steering feel, and mid-corner grip. Your two primary tools for adjustment here are camber plates and offset control arm bushings. For more extreme applications, adjustable lower control arms are the next step.

### Camber & Caster Adjustment

**Camber Plates** are the most common and effective way to adjust front camber and, in some cases, caster. They replace the stock rubber upper strut mount with a metal plate and a spherical bearing, allowing you to slide the top of the strut assembly inboard/outboard (for camber) and fore/aft (for caster).

| **Adjustable LCAs** | Wisefab, IRP (Individual Racing Parts), SLR Speed | €800 - €1,600+ | Drift/track-specific. Allows for massive camber, caster, and track width adjustments. Overkill for a street car. |

On our 328i track project, we started with a set of Silver Project camber plates. The installation is straightforward while the coilovers are out. We were able to easily achieve -3.5° of camber, which is impossible with the stock top mounts. For caster, we pressed in E36 M3 3.2L offset Front Control Arm Bushings (FCABs). This combination provides a huge range of adjustment for track use while being completely reversible.

**Caster is crucial.** Stock non-M E36s have around 3-4° of positive caster. Pushing this to 6-8° (achievable with offset bushings and adjustable plates) dramatically improves high-speed stability and gives the steering a much more positive, self-centering feel, especially exiting a corner.

## Rear Axle: Taming the Z-Axle

The E36 rear end is prone to excessive negative camber and toe-in when lowered. This is great for stability but terrible for tire wear and rotation. To correct this, you need adjustable rear control arms.

### Rear Camber and Toe Control

The factory eccentric bolts run out of adjustment range very quickly. On a car lowered by 50mm or more, you'll likely be stuck with -2.5° or more of rear camber, which is often too much for a balanced setup.

**Adjustable Rear Upper Control Arms (Camber Arms)** are the solution. They replace the stock, non-adjustable upper arm (part no. 33326770813) with a turnbuckle-style arm, allowing you to lengthen or shorten it to precisely set rear camber.

When installing adjustable arms, focus on quality. Cheaper arms often use low-quality heim joints that wear out quickly, leading to clunking and unpredictable handling. We opted for Hardrace arms on our build, which use hardened rubber or spherical pillowball bushings for a good balance of performance and durability.

The installation process involves dropping the rear shocks to access the inner bolts and using the factory eccentric bolt for coarse adjustment and the new arm for fine-tuning. Always get a professional alignment immediately after installation.

## Alignment Specs: Recipes for Success

With your adjustable parts installed, it's time to visit the alignment rack. The "right" specs depend entirely on your intended use, tire choice, and personal preference. Below are three proven starting points.

**Important:** These values are per side. Total toe is double the per-side value.

| Parameter | Spirited Street / Weekend Canyon | Track Day / Grip | Pro-Level Drift |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Front Camber** | -1.5° to -2.0° | -3.0° to -4.0° | -4.0° to -6.0° |
| **Front Caster** | +6.0° to +7.5° | +7.0° to +8.5° | +7.0° to +8.5° |
| **Front Toe** | 0.05° Toe In | 0.00° (Zero) to 0.05° Toe Out | 0.05° to 0.15° Toe Out |
| **Rear Camber** | -1.0° to -1.5° | -1.8° to -2.5° | 0.00° to -0.5° |
| **Rear Toe** | 0.10° Toe In | 0.10° to 0.15° Toe In | 0.00° (Zero) to 0.10° Toe In |

### Analysis of Setups:

*   **Spirited Street:** This setup is the perfect compromise. It provides a significant improvement in turn-in and grip over stock without causing excessive inner tire wear. The slight toe-in front and rear maintains good highway stability.
*   **Track Day / Grip:** This is an aggressive setup focused on maximizing the tire's contact patch during hard cornering. The high negative front camber keeps the outside tire flat to the pavement mid-corner. Zero or slight toe-out at the front makes the car very eager to turn in, while the rear toe-in provides stability under power on corner exit. Expect faster tire wear, especially on the street.
*   **Pro-Level Drift:** This is a specialized setup. The massive front camber and caster, combined with toe-out, creates huge front-end grip even at extreme steering angles. The near-zero rear camber maximizes the rear contact patch for forward bite while sliding. This setup feels terrible for normal driving but is essential for competitive drifting. Note: Achieving these specs often requires drift-specific knuckles (like Wisefab or SLR) to manage Ackermann angle and prevent binding at full lock.

## Advanced Geometry: Bump Steer & Roll Center

For those building a dedicated track or race car, two more concepts are worth considering.

*   **Bump Steer Correction:** When you lower an E36 aggressively, the angle of the steering tie rod no longer matches the angle of the lower control arm. This causes the wheel to steer itself as the suspension moves up and down. To fix this, you can install **adjustable outer tie rod ends** that allow you to change the mounting point height, correcting the geometry. Kits from SLR Speed or Race-Skids are popular options, typically costing €300+.
*   **Roll Center Correction:** Lowering a car also lowers its roll center, the geometric point around which the car's body rolls. A roll center that is too low can lead to excessive body roll. **Roll center correction kits**, which usually consist of extended ball joints and new outer tie rod ends, raise the roll center back to a more favorable position, improving chassis response.

These are advanced modifications and are generally not necessary for a street or light track-day car. However, for a sub-100mm ride height, they become critical for predictable handling.

## What's Next?

Dialing in your suspension geometry is one of the most impactful mods you can make to an E36. It's the foundation upon which all other handling modifications are built. Once your alignment is set, the next logical steps are to address the other weak points in the system. Consider upgrading your sway bars and end links to control body roll, and replace the 25-year-old rubber bushings throughout the car with polyurethane or solid mounts to further sharpen response. With the right geometry, your E36 will feel more planted, predictable, and rewarding to drive than ever before.
