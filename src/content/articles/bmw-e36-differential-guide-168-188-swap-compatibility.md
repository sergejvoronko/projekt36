*   **Why:** Swapping to a shorter differential ratio is the most effective mechanical upgrade for improving acceleration, while adding a Limited Slip Differential (LSD) transforms rear-end grip.
*   **Effort:** A weekend, requiring a jack and transmission lift, pry bars, and a torque wrench.

## Identifying Case Sizes: 168mm, 188mm, and 210mm

BMW utilized three distinct differential case sizes across the E36 generation, categorized by the diameter of the internal ring gear. Identifying what is currently under the car is the mandatory first step before attempting any swap.

*   **168mm (Small Case):** Found almost exclusively in 4-cylinder models (316i, 318i, 318is) and a handful of early 320i models. These are physically smaller, lighter, and visually identifiable by a rear cover that typically utilizes 6 bolts. While adequate for factory 4-cylinder output, the small case is a known failure point when subjected to high-torque engine swaps or forced induction.
*   **188mm (Medium Case):** The standard differential for 6-cylinder E36 chassis (323i, 325i, 328i, and US-spec 3.0L/3.2L M3s). Identified by an 8-bolt rear cover, the 188mm case is highly robust and widely considered capable of handling naturally aspirated V8 swaps and moderate boost without issue.
*   **210mm (Large Case):** Exclusive to the Euro-spec E36 M3 3.2 (EVO). This case is physically massive and features a completely different mounting structure. Swapping a 210mm differential into a standard E36 requires the Euro M3 rear subframe, trailing arms, and a matching driveshaft.

**The E36 Compact Exception:** 
The E36 Compact (318ti, 323ti) and the BMW Z3 utilize an older semi-trailing arm rear suspension design inherited from the E30 generation. Differentials from standard E36 sedans, coupes, convertibles, and tourings will not bolt into an E36 Compact. 

## Factory Gear Ratios and Selecting an Upgrade

The differential gear ratio dictates how engine RPM translates to wheel speed. A "shorter" ratio (a numerically higher number, like 3.91) multiplies torque for aggressive acceleration but results in higher cruising RPMs on the highway. A "taller" ratio (a numerically lower number, like 2.93) provides relaxed highway cruising and higher top speed in gear but numbs acceleration.

When altering your ratio, you must account for your transmission gearing. For a deep dive into transmission variants, consult the [BMW E36 Gearbox Selection, ZF, Getrag, and What to Use for an M50 Swap](/guides/e36-gearbox-selection-swap) guide. 

Below are the most common factory E36 differential ratios:

| Model | Transmission | Gear Ratio | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **328i** | Manual | 2.93 | Very tall gearing; optimized for fuel economy over acceleration. |
| **325i** | Manual | 3.15 | A balanced ratio, commonly utilized as a modest upgrade for 328i owners. |
| **M3 (US 3.0L)** | Manual | 3.15 | Standard equipment for 1995 US M3s. |
| **M3 (US 3.2L)** | Manual | 3.23 | Standard equipment for 1996-1999 US M3s. |
| **M3 (US)** | Automatic | 3.38 | Highly sought-after upgrade for manual cars; offers aggressive acceleration while remaining streetable. |
| **318is** | Manual | 3.45 | Standard 4-cylinder ratio in a 168mm case. |
| **325i / 328i** | Automatic | 3.91 | Extremely short. When paired with a manual gearbox, first gear becomes almost obsolete. |

On a standard 328i manual, swapping from the factory 2.93 differential to a 3.15 or 3.23 unit will yield a highly noticeable improvement in acceleration through gears 1-3 without rendering 5th gear unusable on the highway.

## Output Flanges and Axle Compatibility

One of the most frequent hurdles when sourcing a differential from a different E36 model is axle compatibility. M3 axles feature thicker CV joints and a larger bolt pattern than non-M 325i or 328i axles. If you attempt to bolt a 328i axle to an M3 differential, the hardware will not line up.

Fortunately, the internal splines where the output flanges lock into the 188mm differential carrier are identical across all E36 188mm differentials. The output flanges are secured inside the differential solely by spring-tensioned circlips. 

**The Swap Procedure:**
1. Using a large pry bar positioned carefully against the differential housing, apply sharp, outward leverage behind the output flange. 
2. The flange will pop out of the housing along with its internal circlip. 
3. Remove the M3 output flanges and set them aside.
4. Pry the output flanges out of your original non-M 188mm differential.
5. Snap your original non-M flanges into the new M3 differential. Ensure they seat fully and lock into place.
6. Your factory non-M axles will now bolt directly to the upgraded differential.

## The Input Flange Warning: Preload and Crush Sleeves

While output flanges are highly interchangeable, the input flange (where the driveshaft bolts to the differential) is a completely different engineering environment. 

E36 driveshafts utilize either a 4-bolt or 6-bolt CV flange at the differential. If you purchase an M3 differential with a 6-bolt input flange for your 4-bolt 328i driveshaft, it is incredibly tempting to simply buzz the large pinion nut off with an impact gun and swap the input flanges.

**Do not do this without understanding the mechanics.** The large nut securing the input flange determines the preload on the pinion bearings. Behind the front bearing sits a crush sleeve. From the factory, this nut is tightened until the sleeve crushes to a highly specific tolerance, setting the resistance of the pinion shaft. 

If you remove the pinion nut and swap the flange, you release this tension. Re-torquing the nut blindly risks either under-tightening it (resulting in differential whine and rapid bearing failure) or over-tightening it (crushing the sleeve further and seizing the bearings). If an input flange swap is mandatory, the correct mechanical procedure involves disassembling the pinion shaft, installing a brand-new crush sleeve, and setting the rotating torque to precise BMW workshop manual specifications. 

The safer, more reliable alternative is to source the rear half of an E36 driveshaft that matches the input flange of your new differential. E36 driveshafts utilize a splined slip-joint in the center; unthreading the collar allows you to slide the rear half off and slide on a matching section.

## Swapping from a Small Case (168mm) to Medium Case (188mm)

If you are upgrading a 4-cylinder chassis to a 6-cylinder powerplant, retaining the 168mm small case differential is a liability. Swapping to the 188mm medium case is mandatory for reliability, but the differential alone is not a drop-in replacement. 

Because the 188mm housing is physically longer and wider than the 168mm housing, the surrounding drivetrain geometry changes. To execute this swap, you will need a complete package:

*   **188mm Differential:** Sourced from a 323i, 325i, or 328i.
*   **Medium Case Axles:** The 168mm axles are slightly longer to bridge the gap to the narrower small case differential. Attempting to force 168mm axles onto a 188mm differential will bind the CV joints and destroy them. You must source factory 6-cylinder E36 axles. 
*   **Medium Case Driveshaft (Rear Section):** The 188mm differential protrudes further forward toward the transmission. The rear section of the driveshaft must be the shorter, 6-cylinder version to bolt up without bottoming out the center slip-joint.

Both case sizes share the same front differential mounting bolt location on the subframe, meaning the subframe itself does not need to be replaced when jumping from 168mm to 188mm.

## Identifying Limited Slip Differentials (LSD)

The factory E36 LSD utilizes a clutch-type (Salisbury) mechanical lock, natively offering 25% lockup. Finding one in a salvage yard or classified ad requires verification, as many sellers misidentify standard open differentials.

**1. The Tag Check:**
Clean the heavy grime off the metal identification tag attached to one of the upper rear cover bolts. A factory LSD will have a stamped "S" preceding the gear ratio (e.g., **S3.15** or **S3.23**). An open differential will simply list the ratio or have no prefix (e.g., **3.15**).

**2. The Spin Test:**
Tags can be swapped. To verify the internals mechanically, place the differential on a bench (or raise the rear of the car securely). 
*   Spin one output flange forward by hand.
*   If the opposite output flange rotates in the **same direction**, the differential has a mechanical limited slip unit.
*   If the opposite output flange rotates in the **opposite direction**, it is an open differential.

*Note: The spin test confirms the presence of an LSD, but not its health. A heavily worn factory clutch pack may act like an open differential under load.*
